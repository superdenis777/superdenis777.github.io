老子 · 道德經

Laozi, the Dao De Jing, as a globe: 道德球 Dao De Qiu. Version 2.0, 13 November 2026.

The globe carries the whole of the Laozi, also called the Dao De Jing: 81 chapters, 5,286 characters, cut as one spiral groove from chapter 1 at the north pole to chapter 81 at the south. Turn it, and every character that crosses the middle is plucked in its own Mandarin tone. Let go, and the passage under the lens rises and is read: Chinese, then English, one passage at a time, 201 passages in all. The character 無, “nothing”, is never sung. It is a rest.

The Chinese text

Wang Bi’s text (王弼本), as printed in the Zhengtong Daozang of 1445 (DZ 690), from the Kanseki Repository’s digital edition, Kanripo KR5c0073 (commit b988441, branch HFL). Wang Bi (226–249) wrote the commentary through which the book was read for most of its history, and his text is what scholars mean by “the received text”.

In that print, text and commentary run together. The main text was separated by aligning it against each chapter, and every disputed place was checked by hand. Ten obvious copying errors in the print are not followed; they are listed below. Characters are given in standard traditional forms. Where the print writes a variant or loan form of the same word (田 for 畋, 怕 for 泊, 忽 for 惚, 弊 for 敝, 澹 for 淡, 汎 for 氾, 大 for 太, 飢 for 饑, 惟 for 唯), the standard form is used. The punctuation is modern.

A second copy of Wang Bi, the Zhang edition (華亭張氏本) as transcribed on Wikisource, was used only as a check, because that transcription has been edited toward modern readings in places. In eleven places the two copies disagree and the globe follows the Daozang. Those places, marked Daozang below, should be checked against Lou Yulie’s critical edition, 《王弼集校釋》 (Zhonghua, 1980).

Chapter titles are Heshang Gong’s, from the Daozang’s plain text, DZ 664 (Kanripo KR5c0045). The chapter numbers are the received arrangement. The oldest copies have none.

The English

A new plain translation made for this globe in 2026 with Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, and revised in November 2026 to follow Wang Bi’s text line by line. It translates the Chinese printed here, passage by passage. Where it chooses between readings, it takes the one this Chinese text supports.

Other editions, beside each passage

After a passage has been read, a square shows the same lines in other editions wherever they read differently. Characters that differ from Wang Bi are in red. □ is a character lost from the silk. A red · marks a word Wang Bi has and that edition lacks. Variant forms of the same word (the silk’s 亓 for 其, 胃 for 謂) are shown as written but not marked. Where a difference changes the sense, one plain line says how. There are 31 such notes, and each was checked against the edition it cites.
